For Fiona & Agnes Martin: 'I Love the Whole World'

Suppose I described to you a car ride from my childhood. Halfway between Colorado and New Mexico, my memory from this time largely involves petrol stations and fast food meals, my interspersing slumber frequently interrupted by my brother’s constant anguish at the prospect of any necessitated further patience. Mile after mile on the darkened highway, every second seemed to stretch into infinity, the surrounding landscape blurring in and out of existence as we hurtled further into the American unknown, a nuclear model propelled into the mythic slipstream of the landscape we found ourselves amongst.

And then, slow-blinking in the small, white bedroom of a roadside motel. Dust has blown in from the desert on the horizon, an amber haze that has settled atop the striped sheets that ensconced me. The sun sinks behind the hills, the television set flickers, and my vision draws to a close, the bright glow of the boxy screen forming a grid-like imprint on the back of my eyelids. Retinas burning in the dark room, it was an index of something more-than, something beautiful and beyond me. It is impossible to place the feeling of this moment, so hard to articulate the importance of this eidetic kaleidoscope, of what it meant to me in the what was then and the what cannot possibly be again. The memory feels largely fabricated now, its edges worn soft and slippery with time, the decades since this near-platonic roadtrip culminating in an erosion of the real, of the objective. Situating oneself within the memory requires a dip back into the nonobjective, the feeling, the fleeting existence of a smaller, younger self. 

It is maybe the next day, and we are in what is maybe Arizona. My brother pulls on my mother’s arm. He wants to fling himself over the edge and into the clay abyss below us. I bellow something that was probably ECHO! but also could have been anything. I do not remember. What I do remember is the sound of my voice as it reverberated through the canyon, a long, carrying thing that bounced off every jagged surface, every non-visible face of this landmark, so incomprehensibly vast, the largest thing I had ever seen in my life. It was sublime, almost impossibly so, and it exists so pertinently outside my current existence that I almost believe that I made it up, a fictive void that lies only within my imaginary. 

Suppose I now describe to you a walk I took in late Autumn, two months into the first isolation. The air is cold, crisp, and smells of wood smoke, a signifier of the cool change that had swept across the valley and then proceeded to settle in, a serene chill as ubiquitous as the gum trees that swayed above me. I recall the dew that had collected on the long grass and the thin, blue lines it made on my jeans as I waded through it, my shoes damp and muddy as I made my way back onto the foot path. My home no more than a mirage on the horizon, I turned my gaze skyward and then to nothing, feeling myself dissipate into my surroundings, my body no more than the mark of my shadow as it stained the pavement underfoot. 

Exactly three years later and I am waltzing through North Melbourne, picking leaves out of another’s hair. A flash of a smile, a hand in mine, the bluestone slick with morning rain. Watch your step here. And talking, talking forever, so much to say and in so little time, even if the hourglass had not yet inverted. Knowing someone so well that you feel as though you could sink into them, your existences symbiotic and endlessly intertwined. This feeling, this experience, is so beautiful, soft, and sentimental, there is no language I could use that would adequately portray the tender rhythms of the heart. Every time I use my words they seem to fail, their cover compromised like a well-worn pair of jeans, or a second-hand blanket: gappy, riddled with holes, and dampened by the dew of daybreak. Love, it seems, is not didactic, but rather contained within one’s interior: someplace deep within the spirit, nestled at the very centre of the soul. 

This is the role of art: to fill in the gaps, to explain the inexplicable. It captures the essence, the idyll, and allows us to stay in it, an encapsulation of the spiritual and beautiful, the fleeting rendered permanent, even if only for as long as it is beholden. Gazing upon Agnes Martin’s paintings, one is transfixed by the paths and currents of life, all of the crossroads and sudden forks that have occurred for you to be here, in the Now, standing in front of an artwork. Art therefore marks the multiplicity of our existences: the artist made real through their construction of a visible, material form, and the viewer through their response to this objective, to the steady thrum of one’s heart as it finds the “vanishing point” of the work: the moment when the temporal blurs and the spirit transcends the physical. When I run my fingers across a piece of cracked leather, for example, I am reminded of my grandfather’s chair, every divot an acknowledgement of age, of history, of who had once sat there. Naturally, what follows: his quiet presence in the corner, the labour of his breath, the loud rumble of his machine in the nighttime, the three sugars he took in his coffee. His essence contained within the tactile, this weathered fabric becomes more than its objective form, but rather the material proof of his life, and of mine, as I look, and look, and remember. A temporal mark of the what-was and what-is, it is art that permits us to access the beauty and wonder of the world, a rendering of the non-objective, of the spirit that resides within us all.
